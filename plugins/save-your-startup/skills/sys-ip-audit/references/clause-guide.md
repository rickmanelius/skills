# Clause guide: what to read for, in plain words

Not legal advice. This is a reading list, so the clauses are already found when the user and their lawyer sit
down and the hour goes to judgment. Record each one in the ledger's "Agreements in play" table, with the section number and the exact
words, and mark anything unclear **[confirm]**.

Read every agreement in play, including older ones: an earlier NDA with the same counterparty, a
past employer's invention assignment that may still reach this work, a client NDA that covers the same field.

## The prior-inventions clause itself

Usually a short paragraph pointing to an exhibit or schedule ("Exhibit A", "Schedule A", "Prior Inventions").
Look for:

- **What happens if no list is attached.** Common forms say it outright: "if no such list is attached, I
  represent that there are no such Prior Inventions." That's the whole reason "None" is expensive.
- **What counts.** "Inventions, original works of authorship, developments, improvements and trade secrets" is
  typical. That's broader than patents: code, writing, designs and know-how count.
- **Confidentiality carve-out.** Most forms let you describe something held in confidence for someone else in
  general terms only, by the owner's role and a general field. Use it. Naming a client's project here can breach
  their NDA.
- **The license on incorporation.** Common forms say that if you build a prior invention into a company product
  or process, the company gets a "nonexclusive, royalty-free, irrevocable, perpetual, worldwide license" to it,
  often with the right to sublicense. So list everything, and decide on purpose which prior work goes into the new
  work.

## Effective date and reach-back

- **Effective date.** The day the agreement says it starts, which may not be the day it's signed.
- **Reach-back.** Does "confidential information" include what was disclosed *before* the effective date? Many
  NDAs say so ("whether disclosed before or after the date of this agreement"). If it does, everything from the
  first private disclosure is covered, and the first-disclosure date matters more.
- **Assignment reach-back.** Does the invention assignment cover work from before the start date, or from "the
  period of my relationship with the company" (which may have started with unpaid help)? This is where unpaid
  contributions (bucket C) get swept in or left floating.

## Independent development

The NDA's exclusion for information "independently developed by the recipient without use of or reference to"
the other side's confidential information. Bucket B lives or dies here. To claim it, the user needs to show:
own time, own equipment, own accounts, and none of the other side's material. Note the exact wording; some say
"as shown by contemporaneous written records", which raises the evidence bar.

## What's assigned, and from when

- **Scope.** "Related to the company's business or actual or demonstrably anticipated research" is common. The
  broader the counterparty's field is described, the more of the user's side work it reaches. Tailor the
  schedule to that wording.
- **State carve-outs.** Several US states limit how far an employer can reach into inventions made on the
  employee's own time without company equipment, supplies, facilities or trade secrets. California's is Labor
  Code section 2870; Delaware, Illinois, Kansas, Minnesota, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, Utah and
  Washington have similar laws, and Nevada has a different kind. Each has exceptions, usually for work that
  relates to the employer's business or comes from work done for the employer. Note the governing law and
  whether the agreement attaches the state's notice. The lawyer confirms how it applies.
- **After the relationship ends.** Some forms presume that inventions disclosed within a period after leaving
  were made during the relationship. Note the period.

## Ownership and license language in NDAs

An NDA usually transfers nothing, but read for:

- **Feedback.** "Any feedback or suggestions you provide may be used by us without restriction." If present, the
  user's ideas shared in meetings may already be licensed or given away. Flag it.
- **Residuals.** Lets the recipient use what stays in their unaided memory. Cuts both ways. Flag it.
- **No license implied.** Usually harmless; confirms nothing transfers.

## Joint work

Does any agreement say who owns work made together? Silence matters too: without an agreement, jointly made work
may be jointly owned, and each owner's rights depend on the jurisdiction (a question for the lawyer). That ambiguity
is usually what the brief's Part A asks to settle.

## Return or destroy

What must happen to the other side's material when the relationship ends, and whether the user's copies (session
logs, notes, repos, AI tool histories) are covered. The self-audit checks where those copies live.

## Governing law, venue, assignment of the agreement

- **Governing law and venue.** Which state's or country's law applies, and where disputes are heard. It decides
  which carve-outs apply.
- **Who can assign the agreement.** Can the counterparty hand the user's assignment to an acquirer, or to a
  different entity? Note it; Part C asks which entity takes the assignment.

## Signatures

Who signed, on which date, in what capacity, and whether the other side has countersigned. An unsigned
counterpart goes at the top of the list of things to raise.
