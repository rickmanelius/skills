# MOU

**Companion skill, not from the book.** The format and the core idea come from Michael Girdley ([@girdley](https://x.com/girdley)), in his X thread "When people start something with partners..." (April 21, 2023): https://x.com/girdley/status/1649393986899959813. His post, his answers to readers, and his sample MOU are in `references/`. This skill adds an interview up front and a clarity loop that runs until the draft is ready to send.

> "If someone can't have a tough conversation at the beginning, they definitely won't later on!" (Michael Girdley)

## Why This Matters

Partnerships rarely blow up over the idea. They blow up over what nobody wrote down: who does what, how many hours, whose money, who decides, and what happens when someone wants out. Each partner fills those gaps with their own assumptions, and both walk away sure they agreed.

Mike's fix is a short Memorandum of Understanding, written before any lawyer is involved, that puts down "What's the idea/vision," "Who's doing what," and "What's our plan." He has used it on about 15 partnerships and says none has failed because of misalignment. When memories differ later, "We just pull out the document and say, 'Well, this is what it says right here.'"

That only works if the page says one thing. A memo each partner can read their own way is worse than no memo, because now both of them have a quote.

## The Key Insight

> Clear beats complete.

Formal agreements come later. In Mike's words, "Everything gets papered eventually," and with a good MOU in hand "that's just a formality at that point." The MOU has a narrower job: make sure both partners picture the same deal, in plain words, on two pages. Every line should mean the same thing to you, to your partner, and to a stranger reading it three years from now. A line that can be read two ways will be argued two ways.

## AI-Era Callout

AI makes a 22-page founders' agreement free to produce. (Mike, on finding one online: "Did a lawyer write that template?!") Length no longer signals diligence, and a long document mostly guarantees that nobody reads it closely. Use AI here for the two things that are still hard: asking the uncomfortable questions, and reading the draft the way your partner will. Keep the document itself short enough that both of you read every word.

## The Two-Page Shape

Page 1 is the deal. Page 2 is the plan. The fill-in template is `templates/mou-template.md`.

| Section | What it answers |
|---|---|
| **Vision** | What are we building, for whom, and how big? |
| **How we will run it** | What are we optimizing for? (Mike calls these Organizational Design Principles.) |
| **Who puts in what** | Money, hours, assets, and what each person wants out of it, side by side |
| **Who owns what** | Which outcomes and decisions belong to each person |
| **Structure** | Entity, ownership and the reason for it, decisions, money out, exit |
| **First rocks** | Each person's first few finished outcomes, with dates |
| **Open questions** | What is not agreed yet, who closes it, by when |
| **What this is** | Not a contract; when it gets papered; an agreed-in-principle line |

**Length.** Aim for 400 to 600 words and stop at 750. Mike's page 1 is 365 words and fills the page. When a draft runs long, cut words before sections. If it still runs long, you may have two deals in one memo.

**What changed from Mike's sample, and why.** Contributions and responsibilities are split apart, because what you put in and what you own are different questions. Each person says what they want out of it. Unsettled points move into one list instead of sitting mid-sentence. Real names replace "Partner #1". The reasoning, with examples from his sample, is at the end of `references/girdley-mou-sample.md`.

## How This Works

Mike's own process: "We usually just talk it through, then one person writes it out, shares it, then have another meeting to finalize/discuss the results." This skill is the "one person writes it out" step, with the hard questions asked first and a clarity check before the draft leaves your hands.

There are five phases. Take them one at a time and ask a few questions per round, never the whole list at once.

### Phase 1: Set the Scene

Ask:

- **What is the opportunity, and who is the partner?** Their name, what they bring, what they are like to work with.
- **Where are things now?** Just an idea, talked through, or already operating with nothing in writing.
- **What already exists?** Notes, a call transcript, emails, an earlier draft. If it is a file, read it.
- **What worries you most?** The thing you suspect the two of you see differently is usually the paragraph the MOU needs most.

Then pick the entry mode:

- **Interview first** (default). Go section by section through Phase 2, then draft.
- **Notes first.** If the user hands over notes and wants a draft quickly, write v1 from what is there and run the interview as part of the clarity loop. Sort every gap (see "Sorting the gaps" below) instead of guessing. A guessed number in a document that is about to be shared turns into a number someone anchors on.
- **Already operating.** Start from current reality: who actually does what today, the hours, the money in so far. Then ask where it should go. Frame the document as "how we work from here", which is far easier to raise with a partner than "let's renegotiate". When today's reality and the current split disagree (equal shares, unequal hours), record that as an open question to discuss, not as a conclusion.

### Phase 2: Interview

Work section by section. Use the questions below as a menu, not a script, and skip what the user has already answered.

**Vision**
- In one sentence: what are we building, for whom, and how big could it get?
- What does success look like in three years, as a number?
- If it takes more than two sentences, there may be two visions. Which one is this memo about?

**How we will run it**
- Profit: reinvest it or pay it out? For how long?
- Involvement: who is full time, part time, or passive, and does that change at some point?
- Risk: how much can we lose before we stop or rethink?
- Is there anything we have agreed we will not do?

**Who puts in what** (for each person)
- Money: how much, when, in what tranches, and what triggers the next tranche? Equity or a loan?
- Time: how many hours a month, starting when, for how long? "A lot" and "as needed" are not answers yet, and neither is "full time": 40 hours a week and 60 are different deals.
- Other: assets, IP, customers, relationships, equipment, a location. Who owns each one afterward?
- Expertise: turn it into hours or deliverables. Mike: "contributing 'expertise' is really tough to quantify."
- Wants out of this: cash flow, a sale, a salary, a title, learning? By when? (A reader under Mike's post asked exactly this: "What do you expect to get out of it?")

**Who owns what** (for each person)
- Which outcomes does each person own, and which decisions can they make alone?
- What does each person explicitly not own, or hand off later? (Mike's sample hands site development to the GM after the first build.)

**Structure**
- Entity: is one needed yet, and of what type? Record the intent and who confirms it with a CPA or attorney. Mike himself flagged the word "federal" in his sample's "federal C-corp" as imprecise, and a tax attorney in the replies pushed back on the C-corp default. Entity choice is a professional's call.
- Ownership: the split, and the one reason for it. If someone brings sweat and no cash, Mike's default is vesting over several years, with their hours stated.
- Decisions: what needs both partners, and how is a tie broken?
- Money out: salaries, distributions, or reinvest, and until when?
- Exit: a buy-sell with a clock (Mike's sample uses a push-pull clause, 30 days to respond and 90 to close). What happens if someone stops putting in their hours? Note that death and disability get handled in the formal agreement.

**First rocks**
- What three to five things does each person finish first? Write each as a finished outcome with a date: "Signed lease for the first location by March 31", not "Work on locations".
- Does a first hire get a column?
- If there are no rocks with dates yet, ask the user for them. Drafting without them, leave the section out rather than filling it with placeholders.

**Open questions**
- What has not been agreed? Each item gets an owner, and the list gets the date of the meeting where you settle it. If no date is set, propose one a week or two out, mark it "(proposed)" in the MOU, and ask the user to confirm it.

**Handling the answers**
- When an answer is vague, ask once more with something concrete to react to: "Closer to 5 hours a month or 20?" If it is still unknown, sort it (below). "I don't know" is useful; it just has to land somewhere.
- The user speaks for themselves. For the partner's side, write what was discussed. The cover note asks the partner to mark anything that reads differently, so the MOU needs no "to confirm" tags.
- Kind over nice. If the deal looks lopsided, or the user keeps steering around a topic, say so plainly, once, and let them decide.

**Sorting the gaps.** Every missing piece goes to one of two places, and only one of them is the MOU.
- **Yours to answer.** Anything the user can settle alone: their own goals, their own hours, facts they hold (what their day-job contract says, what was said on the call, the real numbers). Ask the user. If they are not around, list these at the top of your reply under "Before this goes to [partner]", most important first. They never go into the MOU as questions: the partner should not receive a page of the user's unfinished homework. When the missing piece is a number the MOU needs (the user's own hours, say), leave a visible blank in the draft, such as "__ hours a month", and put it first on the list. A blank is honest and hard to miss; a vague phrase in its place hides the gap from both of you, and the draft is not ready to send until every blank is filled.
- **Decide together.** Real decisions neither partner can make alone: vesting, the exit, decision rights, what the money buys. These go into the MOU's Open questions, because naming them is part of the deal.

**Six open questions at most, one decision each.** Folding three questions into one line to get under the cap defeats the point. A memo that is mostly questions is not a memo yet; it is an agenda. If more than six real joint decisions remain, say so plainly: the conversation has to come before the document. Offer to turn the list into a one-page agenda for that conversation, and write the MOU after it.

### Phase 3: Draft

Fill the template and save it right away as `mou-{slug}.md`, in `deliverables/` if that folder exists or the current directory if not. The file always holds the latest draft, so nothing is lost if the conversation ends. Then go straight to Phase 4.

Order the reply so the user can act on it in the time they have: the "Before this goes to [partner]" questions first (eight at most, most important first), then the full draft, then the clarity report, then the sign-off question.

These are the simplicity and clarity rules. Each one exists because of a specific way partners end up reading the same page differently.

1. **Numbers over adjectives.** Dollars, hours a month, percentages, dates. Adjectives stretch to fit whoever is reading.
2. **State each fact once.** A split given as both a ratio and dollar amounts will drift. Mike's sample says 60/40 "in line with $ contributed" next to $100k and $60k, which is 62.5/37.5.
3. **A name and a verb in every commitment.** "Ana signs the lease by March 31," not "A lease will be secured." Passive voice hides who owes what.
4. **One term for one thing.** Pick "the company" or its name and keep it. Spell out any acronym the partner might not know, or drop it.
5. **No escape words.** Reasonable, fair, help, support, as needed, expected to, best efforts, ASAP, significant, involved. Replace each with a number; without one, sort it as a gap (yours to answer, or decide together).
6. **Nothing unresolved in the body.** No TBD, no bracketed notes, no question marks. Sort those as gaps too.
7. **Plain words.** No "shall", "hereinafter", "whereas" or "party". Write it the way you would say it across a table.
8. **One idea per bullet.** If a bullet needs a second sentence, it is probably two bullets or a table row.
9. **Plain punctuation.** Periods, commas, colons. No em or en dashes: they tend to smuggle a second idea into a line, and many readers now take them as a sign the text was machine-written.
10. **Real names**, never "Partner #1".
11. **Estimates and qualifiers stay as given.** "Sam estimates 25 hours a week for six months" must not become "Sam delivers in six months", and "50/50 for now" keeps its "for now" (or becomes an open question about when to revisit it). The same goes for the trigger in "What this is": the user names when the lawyers come in; don't pick one that holds up money or work. A promise nobody made, sitting in a shared document, is the fastest way to lose the partner's trust in the rest of it.
12. **Stay in budget.** 400 to 600 words, 750 at most.

### Phase 4: Clarity Loop

Run this after every draft: two passes, a short report, then the sign-off question.

**Pass A: the cold read.** Read the draft as the partner, using everything the user has said about them. Find:
- the questions they would ask before agreeing,
- the lines they could read differently from what the user meant ("I took 'help with sales' to mean intros, not a quota"),
- anything that would surprise or sting them. Better to meet it now than in the meeting.

Then read it as a stranger three years from now, say a new investor or a lawyer. From the page alone, could they tell who owes what?

**Your private read.** In one to three sentences, tell the user what a good mentor would: the number that changes the conversation (what 60/40 implies an hour of the partner's work is worth, for example), or the worry under the stated worry ("is this about pay, or about being alone in the building fifty hours a week?"). It goes in the reply and the notes, never in the MOU.

**Pass B: the mechanical check.**
- Word count.
- Money and time for every person, as numbers (or "none").
- Percentages add to 100, and any number that appears twice matches.
- Every rock has an owner and a date. Every open question is one decision with an owner, the list has a settle-by date (proposed is fine), and there are six or fewer.
- Every blank the user must fill is listed at the top of the reply.
- Nothing in Open questions is something the user could answer alone.
- No escape words, inline TBDs, undefined acronyms, legalese or dashes.
- The exit is defined, or it is an open question.

**The report.** Keep it to eight items or fewer, most important first. Don't pad it; if a pass finds nothing, say so.

```
Clarity check, draft v2 (540 words)

My read: if the $30,000 is Brightline's fee, it works out to about $46 an hour for Sam's estimated 650 hours. Settle what that money is before anything else.

Sam will likely ask:
1. Do my 25 hours a week cover support after launch, or only the build?
2. ...

Could be read two ways:
- "Ana leads marketing" -> owns the budget, or does the work? Suggest: "Ana owns the marketing budget and hires the agency."

Mechanical: one miss. "Fair market rent" is an escape word; moved to Open questions.
```

Then ask which fixes to apply and whether the user would decide any of them differently. Make the edits, update the file, and say in a few bullets what changed ("v2 to v3: cut 80 words, gave Sam's hours a number, moved IP ownership to Open questions"). Bump the version in the header every round.

**The sign-off question.** At the end of each round, ask:

> **Would you send this to [partner] exactly as it is?**

Only a clear yes ends the loop. "Mostly", "I think so" or a change of subject means another round, so ask what is holding them back. Also ask: **"Is there a line here you would be uncomfortable reading aloud to [partner]?"** That line is usually the tough conversation Mike says the MOU exists to force. Keep it, and put it on the meeting agenda rather than softening it into something vague.

The user decides when the draft is done, not you. If both passes come back clean and the user still hesitates, say so: the writing is clear, and what is left is a decision. Decisions belong in Open questions or in the meeting, not in another rewrite.

### Phase 5: Ready to Send

When the user says yes:

1. **Finalize `mou-{slug}.md`.** Mark the header "Ready to send, v[N]" with the date. If the user wants .docx or PDF and the tools allow it, convert it and confirm it still fits on two pages.
2. **Write `mou-{slug}-notes.md`**, beside it, with:
   - **A cover note** to the partner, three or four plain sentences: here is my write-up of what we discussed, mark anything that reads differently to you, let's meet on [date] to finalize.
   - **The meeting agenda:** the open questions, plus any line flagged as likely to sting.
   - **For you only:** your private read, and any lawyer or CPA flags from the Ground Rules.
   - **The version log:** what changed in each round, and why.
   - **After the meeting:** blank, for later. What the partner changed, and what the draft missed.
3. **Name the next step.** Mike finishes with a meeting to finalize, so read it together. Afterward, the user can bring the partner's changes back here to make the agreed version, dated, that both partners keep. Revisit it when something big changes (a new partner, a raise, a role shift), and plan to have counsel turn it into formal agreements.

## Ground Rules

- **You are a mentor and a scribe, not a lawyer.** Do not draft legal clauses. When one of these comes up, flag it in one line for a lawyer or CPA, in the reply and the notes, not the MOU:
  - money changing hands before the company exists;
  - founder shares being issued (in the US, the 83(b) tax election must be filed within 30 days, and a miss generally cannot be fixed);
  - work or code created before the company exists, or by someone whose day-job contract may claim it;
  - regulated data, such as health, financial or children's records;
  - a personal guarantee on a lease or loan;
  - anything meant to bind now: confidentiality, exclusivity, IP assignment, the entity and its tax choice.
- **Never invent a term to fill a gap.** Gaps become open questions.
- **Write for the partner.** The test is whether the other side reads the same deal the user meant.
- **The user decides when it is done.**
- **Resistance is data.** If someone will not discuss a topic now, that is the topic. Name it, kindly.
- **Shorter wins ties.**

## Output

By the end, you will have:

- **A one-to-two page MOU** your partner can read in five minutes
- **A clarity report for each draft**, so you can see what got sharper and why
- **A cover note and a meeting agenda** built from the open questions
- **A version log** to show your partner if they ask why something changed
- **Your own sign-off** that it is ready to send
