## Mike's answers in the replies (verbatim)

Each reader question is paraphrased; Mike's answer is quoted exactly. Askers are not named.

**On how the document gets used later** (a reader noted partners change their thinking after big life events):
> I have a limited memory so this is essential for me.
>
> We just pull out the document and say, "Well, this is what it says right here."

**On including how things end** ("a MOU should include how things would end"):
> Agreed.
>
> This made-up one includes the Shotgun clause.
>
> But if you have alignment on the exit strategy, it should be in there too.
>
> Nice point.

**On formal founders' agreements** (a reader called the MOU a simplified founder's agreement):
> Wow. I just googled that and it's... 22 pages?! Did a lawyer write that template?!
>
> :-)

**On shareholder agreements, vesting, drag-along, dispute resolution:**
> Yep. Everything gets papered eventually.
>
> However, that is the stuff that looks like progress but isn't.
>
> I've operated companies like this from zero to 100ks of revenue on a handshake until we're ready to bring in the lawyering.
>
> By then and with this document, that's just a formality at that point.

**On why a C-corp is "simpler to reinvest profits":**
> check out the tax laws for corporations that file as c-corps. It probably shouldn't say federal there for precision.

**On a partner who brings expertise but no money:**
> Usually, you would vest that person over several years.
>
> I like to have hourly contribution quantified here since contributing "expertise" is really tough to quantify.

**On whether resisting the MOU is a red flag:**
> I think so. If someone can't have a tough conversation at the beginning, they definitely won't later on!

**On whether partners should fill it out separately and compare:**
> We usually just talk it through, then one person writes it out, shares it, then have another meeting to finalize/discuss the results.

**On the length** (a reader replied "TLDR"):
> It's like 150 words!

**On the restaurant example:**
> Haha. To be clear, this is a fictitious company.
>
> There is zero way I'm opening a restaurant. Awful business.

## What readers added (paraphrased)

- A reader who said they were a lawyer called it "the best advice I've seen in a while."
- **Ask what each person wants out of it.** One reply simply said: add "What do you expect to get out of it?"
- **It beats a typical operating agreement on substance.** Several readers said an operating agreement or founders' agreement is legalese that does not say who does what, while this does.
- **Hard conversations now or harder ones later.** "If you do not have the hard conversations with prospective biz partners up front, you WILL have hard conversations later when the stresses are already built up."
- **Partners often mean different things by the same plan.** A real-estate reader said would-be partners "have completely different ideas about what real estate investing actually is." Another said many partnerships rest on "unspoken, implicit assumptions."
- **A tax attorney pushed back on the C-corp default:** taxes favor it only while profits are reinvested; early losses get stuck inside it; appreciating assets are better held outside; buyers may pay less in a sale. Entity choice is a question for a CPA or attorney. The MOU records what the partners intend, not the tax answer.
- **Others said the format suits more than founders**, for example a company's first outside executive hire.

## Questions readers asked that Mike did not answer

These are worth covering in the interview because people clearly have them:

- When in the process should the conversation happen, given it takes some of the excitement out of a new project?
- How do you introduce an MOU a year into a partnership that never had one? (One reader suggested starting from current reality, who does what today, then moving to the vision.)
- Should there be a breakup plan in case things go south?
- How do you value sweat equity against cash?
- As the business evolves, do you revise the memo?
- How does the push-pull (shotgun) buy-sell clause work? Plain version, matching Mike's sample: either partner can offer to buy the other out at a price they name. The other must either accept, or turn it around and buy the first partner out on the same terms. Because whoever names the price might end up on either side of the sale, they have a reason to name a fair one. (A lawyer drafts the real clause; the MOU only records that both partners want it and the clock on it.)
